New-Peth Shoes Business Performance Analysis

Why a UK online footwear retailer's busiest category earns the least

Cleaned four years of sales data (2021–2024) in Excel, modelled it in Tableau, and built a KPI dashboard that showed Women's Shoes driving most orders at the lowest margin. The report traces the gap to brand mix, regional discounting and returns, and sets out three recommendations.

58%of orders come from Women's Shoes, at the lowest margin (19%)
54%of Women's sales sit in three heavy-discount regions, at about 16.5% margin
Tools demonstratedTableauExcel
Read full case study

Problem

New-Peth Shoes is a Morpeth-based online footwear retailer selling across 10 UK regions. Its owner wanted a growth plan to compete with high-street stores, but the business had no data-driven view of category, regional or customer performance. The task was to find the levers for profitable growth in four years of transactions.

What I did

In Excel: data preparation

  • Checked every sheet for duplicate rows with Remove Duplicates (none found) and for missing or irrelevant values with Go To Special, deleting one irrelevant record from the Sales sheet.
  • Removed double and trailing spaces, flagging trailing spaces in headers with a RIGHT() test.
  • Standardised capitalisation with PROPER() for names and UPPER() for ID fields, then pasted the results back as values.
  • Validated data types with ISTEXT and ISNUMBER, ran an English (UK) spell check, cleared inherited formats and set a consistent date format.

In Tableau: modelling and dashboard

  • Joined the Sales, Returns, Sales Representatives and Shipping tables.
  • Built calculated fields for Profit-to-Sales Ratio, Average Order Value, Customer Lifetime Value, Profit per Order and Return Rate.
  • Built a dashboard covering category, household segment, region, sales representative performance, and a sales and profit trend with forecast.
  • Drilled into Women's Shoes by brand, region, discount level and return reason.

Dashboard

Findings

  • Volume and margin pull apart. Women's Shoes took 631 orders (58%) but earned a 19% margin and £54 profit per order, against 23% and £189 for Kids' Shoes.
  • Brand mix explains much of the gap. Michael Kors, Hunter and Converse make up 53% of Women's revenue at 15–18% margin, while Lilli Kelly (25%) and Dr Martens (23%) stay under-scaled at 3% and 5% of sales.
  • Discounting depends on region. Midlands, North and Central discount 9–12% and earn 12–19% margin. The seven zero-discount regions earn 20–26%.
  • Returns are a smaller issue. The Women's return rate is 5%, costing about £1,800 in profit. Expectation mismatch (36%) and "changed mind" (21%) account for 57% of returns.

Recommendations

  • Rebalance the brand portfolio. Sort Women's brands into three tiers, renegotiate terms with the high-volume, low-margin suppliers, and put marketing behind high-margin brands. Projected effect: margin up 3–4 percentage points.
  • Govern regional discounts. Set a 20% minimum margin, limit discounts to approved windows, and require an ROI review within 14 days of each promotion. Projected effect: regional margin gap cut by half.
  • Reduce returns. Improve product visualisation with 3D and AR previews, and add a purchase-confidence prompt for impulse orders. Projected effect: return rate from 5% to 4%.

Balancing Cost and Speed

A multi-objective optimisation model for carrier reallocation under supply chain disruption

A multi-objective MILP model, solved in Excel Solver, to help a firm reassign 20 orders after the discontinuation of a carrier — explicitly trading off delivery cost against delivery time across four scenarios.

+0.36%cost for same-day delivery (balanced, α = 0.5)
+6.1%cost of carrier diversification (resilience)
Tools demonstratedExcel SolverMILPMulti-objective optimisation
Read full case study

Problem

When carrier V44_3 was discontinued, 20 affected orders needed reassigning across the remaining feasible carriers. Firms making this call must balance rising logistics costs against rising delivery-speed expectations — and consider how the new allocation distributes risk if disruption strikes again.

Approach

I formulated the problem as a mixed-integer linear program (MILP): each order is assigned to exactly one feasible routing option, with feasibility (product–plant, plant–port, customer restrictions, freight weight-bands) resolved during data preparation. A weighted-sum objective combines cost and time through a parameter α ∈ [0,1] — α = 1 is pure cost minimisation, α = 0 is pure time minimisation — and I solved four scenarios in Excel Solver against a real logistics dataset spanning orders, freight rates, warehouse costs, and routing constraints.

Scenario comparison

ScenarioαTotal costAvg. daysCarrier(s) used
Cost-only1.0$53,992.8214V444_8 ×20
Balanced0.5$54,185.000V444_8 ×20
Time-only0.0$70,748.070V444_0 ×20

Findings & recommendation

  • The balanced solution reaches same-day delivery for only 0.36% above the cheapest possible cost — a rounding error against a 14-day wait.
  • Optimising for time alone is a trap: it reaches the same 0-day delivery as the balanced solution, but costs 31% more, because it ignores carrier rate differences entirely.
  • A single-carrier solution looks optimal on paper but concentrates disruption risk. Capping any one carrier at 50% of volume (the resilience scenario) buys diversification for only 6.1% more.
  • Recommendation: assign orders to the balanced solution by default, and adopt the carrier concentration cap as a standing policy rather than a one-off fix.

FutureLearn Learning Analytics Report

When do learners unenrol, and does it differ by course run?

Applied a two-cycle CRISP-DM process in R to FutureLearn's Cyber Security MOOC (Newcastle University, 7 runs, 37,000+ enrolments), to quantify when learners drop out and identify which runs need retention intervention first.

72.2 daysmedian time to unenrolment (all runs)
24.8%early dropout in the weakest run, vs. 5.8% in the strongest
Tools demonstratedRggplot2CRISP-DMDescriptive statistics
Read full case study

Data cleaning & method

I used R for the whole process, end to end: combining the raw enrolment files from all 7 runs into one dataset, parsing the enrolment/unenrolment timestamps, running data-quality checks, deriving the key variable (days_to_unenrolment), computing the statistics, and building the visualisations. The statistical method throughout is descriptive statistics: median, Q1/Q3, and interquartile range to summarise the typical spread of unenrolment timing, and percentages to quantify early (within-7-day) dropout — visualised with histograms and boxplots.

Cycle 1 — overall baseline

Working from the platform's enrolment records, I filtered to the 4,191 learners with a recorded unenrolment and computed time-to-unenrolment as the gap between enrolling and leaving. Across all seven runs combined, the median was 72.2 days, with a wide interquartile range (30–157 days) — learners disengage throughout the course, not just in the first weeks.

Actionable insights

  • Runs 4–7 (high early dropout): prioritise for onboarding fixes — clearer week-1 expectations and more accessible early content.
  • Runs 1–2 (low early dropout, longer retention): worth investigating what specifically sustained engagement, to replicate it elsewhere.
  • Run 3 (moderate): no immediate action needed; a useful testbed for insights from the other two groups.
  • Longer term: collect more granular data (marketing source, learner motivation), standardise onboarding, and run short exit surveys to capture cohort-specific reasons for leaving.

Limitations

The analysis is descriptive, not causal — it flags where retention differs, not why, since demographic, marketing-channel, and operational data weren't available for comparison. Later runs also have smaller samples, so their estimates should be read with more caution.
